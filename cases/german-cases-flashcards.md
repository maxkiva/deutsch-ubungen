# 🇩🇪 German Cases – Flashcard Set

A detailed practice set covering all four German grammatical cases: **Nominativ, Akkusativ, Dativ, Genitiv**.  
Each card includes the rule, an example, and English commentary explaining *why* it works that way.

---

## 🟦 NOMINATIV (Nominative Case)

---

### Card N-1 — The Subject of the Sentence

**Rule:** The Nominative marks the **subject** — the person or thing *doing* the action.

| Article | Masculine | Feminine | Neuter | Plural |
|---------|-----------|----------|--------|--------|
| Definite | **der** Mann | **die** Frau | **das** Kind | **die** Kinder |
| Indefinite | **ein** Mann | **eine** Frau | **ein** Kind | — Kinder |

**Example:**
> **Der Mann** liest ein Buch.
> *The man reads a book.*

💬 **Commentary:** "Der Mann" is in the Nominative because he is the one performing the action (reading). Think of Nominative as the "actor" case — whoever drives the verb.

---

### Card N-2 — After the verb *sein* (to be)

**Rule:** After *sein*, *werden*, and *bleiben*, both the subject **and** the predicate noun stay in the Nominative.

**Example:**
> Er ist **ein guter Lehrer**.
> *He is a good teacher.*

💬 **Commentary:** Unlike English where this feels natural, learners often expect the "object" to take Accusative here. It doesn't — because there's no action being transferred. Both sides of *sein* are Nominative. Think of it as an equals sign: Er = ein guter Lehrer.

---

## 🟥 AKKUSATIV (Accusative Case)

---

### Card A-1 — The Direct Object

**Rule:** The Accusative marks the **direct object** — the thing being directly acted upon.

| Article | Masculine | Feminine | Neuter | Plural |
|---------|-----------|----------|--------|--------|
| Definite | **den** Mann | **die** Frau | **das** Kind | **die** Kinder |
| Indefinite | **einen** Mann | **eine** Frau | **ein** Kind | — Kinder |

> ⚠️ Only **masculine** changes: *der → den*, *ein → einen*. Feminine, neuter, and plural stay the same!

**Example:**
> Ich sehe **den Mann**.
> *I see the man.*

💬 **Commentary:** The man is being *seen* — he's the recipient of the action, not the doer. Only masculine nouns visibly change in the Accusative, which is why it's the most common "first mistake" for beginners.

---

### Card A-2 — Common Accusative Prepositions

**Rule:** These prepositions **always** take the Accusative:

> **durch** (through) · **für** (for) · **gegen** (against) · **ohne** (without) · **um** (around/at) · **bis** (until) · **entlang** (along)

**Mnemonic:** **D**urch **F**ür **G**egen **O**hne **U**m — *"dfgou"* or the sentence: *„Durch für gegen ohne um"*

**Example:**
> Das Geschenk ist **für meinen Vater**.
> *The gift is for my father.*

💬 **Commentary:** "Vater" is masculine → *mein* becomes *meinen* in Accusative. Memorizing these prepositions as a fixed list saves you a lot of guesswork. They never switch to another case.

---

### Card A-3 — Time Expressions (Accusative)

**Rule:** Definite time spans and repeated times often use the Accusative without a preposition.

**Examples:**
> Ich lerne **jeden Tag** Deutsch.
> *I learn German every day.*

> Er hat **einen Monat** in Berlin gewohnt.
> *He lived in Berlin for a month.*

💬 **Commentary:** These "free" Accusative time expressions are common in everyday speech. No preposition is needed — just put the time phrase in the Accusative. Compare to English "every day" / "for a month" — same structure, no preposition.

## 🟨 DATIV (Dative Case)

---

### Card D-1 — The Indirect Object

**Rule:** The Dative marks the **indirect object** — the person or thing *receiving* or *benefiting from* the action.

| Article | Masculine | Feminine | Neuter | Plural |
|---------|-----------|----------|--------|--------|
| Definite | **dem** Mann | **der** Frau | **dem** Kind | **den** Kindern |
| Indefinite | **einem** Mann | **einer** Frau | **einem** Kind | — Kindern |

> ⚠️ Plural nouns in Dative add **-n** to the end (e.g., Kinder → Kindern, Frauen → Frauen — already ends in -n).

**Example:**
> Ich gebe **dem Kind** ein Buch.
> *I give the child a book.*

💬 **Commentary:** "Dem Kind" is the Dative (indirect) object — the child *receives* the book. "Ein Buch" is the Accusative (direct) object — the thing being given. When in doubt, ask: *To whom? For whom?* → That's your Dative.

---

### Card D-2 — Common Dative Prepositions

**Rule:** These prepositions **always** take the Dative:

> **aus** (from/out of) · **bei** (at/near/with) · **mit** (with) · **nach** (after/to) · **seit** (since/for) · **von** (from/of) · **zu** (to) · **außer** (except) · **gegenüber** (opposite)

**Mnemonic:** **"Aus bei mit nach seit von zu"** — say it like a chant! (+ außer, gegenüber)

**Example:**
> Ich fahre **mit meiner Schwester**.
> *I'm travelling with my sister.*

> Er wohnt **bei seinem Freund**.
> *He is staying at his friend's place.*

💬 **Commentary:** "Schwester" is feminine → *meine* becomes *meiner* in Dative. These prepositions, like Accusative ones, are fixed — they never trigger a different case. Learn both lists and you've eliminated a huge source of errors.

---

### Card D-3 — Two-Way Prepositions (Dative for Location)

**Rule:** These prepositions take **Dative** when expressing *location* (where?) and **Accusative** when expressing *movement toward* (where to?):

> **an** · **auf** · **hinter** · **in** · **neben** · **über** · **unter** · **vor** · **zwischen**

**The trick:** Ask **WO?** (where?) → Dative · Ask **WOHIN?** (where to?) → Accusative

**Examples:**
> Das Buch liegt **auf dem Tisch**. *(Wo? — Dative)*
> *The book is lying on the table.*

> Ich lege das Buch **auf den Tisch**. *(Wohin? — Accusative)*
> *I'm putting the book on the table.*

💬 **Commentary:** This is one of the trickiest points in German. The verb is often your clue: *liegen/stehen/hängen* (to lie/stand/hang — static) → Dative. *legen/stellen/hängen* (to lay/place/hang — movement/putting) → Accusative. Notice "hängen" appears in both — context decides!

---

### Card D-4 — Dative with Verbs (no preposition needed)

**Rule:** Some German verbs take a Dative object directly, with no preposition. These must be memorized.

**Common Dative-only verbs:**
| Verb | Meaning |
|------|---------|
| helfen | to help |
| danken | to thank |
| gehören | to belong to |
| gefallen | to please / to like |
| antworten | to answer |
| glauben | to believe |
| folgen | to follow |
| fehlen | to be missing / to miss |

**Examples:**
> Kannst du **mir** helfen?
> *Can you help me?*

> Das Haus gehört **meinem Onkel**.
> *The house belongs to my uncle.*

> Du fehlst **mir**.
> *I miss you.* (literally: "You are missing to me")

💬 **Commentary:** "Du fehlst mir" is a classic that trips up English speakers. In German, the feeling of missing someone is expressed with *fehlen + Dative* — the person who is missed is the subject! This reversal is a great example of why Dative verbs need special attention.

## 🟩 GENITIV (Genitive Case)

---

### Card G-1 — Possession

**Rule:** The Genitive expresses **possession or belonging**. It corresponds to English *'s* or *of*.

| Article | Masculine | Feminine | Neuter | Plural |
|---------|-----------|----------|--------|--------|
| Definite | **des** Mannes | **der** Frau | **des** Kindes | **der** Kinder |
| Indefinite | **eines** Mannes | **einer** Frau | **eines** Kindes | — Kinder |

> ⚠️ Masculine and neuter nouns add **-s** or **-es** to the noun itself (e.g., Mann → Mannes, Kind → Kindes).

**Example:**
> Das ist das Auto **des Mannes**.
> *That is the man's car.*

> Die Farbe **des Himmels** ist blau.
> *The color of the sky is blue.*

💬 **Commentary:** The Genitive is increasingly replaced in spoken German by *von + Dative* ("das Auto von dem Mann"), especially in informal speech. But the Genitive is still essential in written German, formal contexts, and with certain prepositions. Don't skip it!

---

### Card G-2 — Common Genitive Prepositions

**Rule:** These prepositions **always** take the Genitive:

> **wegen** (because of) · **trotz** (despite) · **während** (during) · **statt / anstatt** (instead of) · **innerhalb** (inside of) · **außerhalb** (outside of) · **aufgrund** (due to) · **mithilfe** (with the help of)

**Examples:**
> **Wegen des Regens** bleiben wir zu Hause.
> *Because of the rain we're staying home.*

> **Trotz des schlechten Wetters** gingen wir spazieren.
> *Despite the bad weather, we went for a walk.*

💬 **Commentary:** In colloquial German, *wegen* and *trotz* are often heard with Dative (*wegen dem Regen*), which is technically incorrect in standard German. In writing and formal speech, always use the Genitive with these prepositions.

---

### Card G-3 — Genitive with Names (Proper Nouns)

**Rule:** With proper names, German simply adds **-s** directly to the name (no apostrophe, unlike English).

**Examples:**
> **Annas** Buch liegt auf dem Tisch.
> *Anna's book is on the table.*

> Das ist **Deutschlands** Hauptstadt.
> *That is Germany's capital.*

💬 **Commentary:** Notice there is **no apostrophe** before the -s in German! An apostrophe *('s)* in German actually signals a missing letter (like in contractions), not possession. Writing "Anna's Buch" is a common error influenced by English. Simply write "Annas Buch."

## 🔁 QUICK COMPARISON TABLE

| Case | Role | Question to ask | Masculine article change |
|------|------|-----------------|--------------------------|
| **Nominativ** | Subject (doer) | *Wer/Was?* (Who/What?) | **der / ein** |
| **Akkusativ** | Direct object | *Wen/Was?* (Whom/What?) | **den / einen** |
| **Dativ** | Indirect object | *Wem?* (To whom?) | **dem / einem** |
| **Genitiv** | Possession | *Wessen?* (Whose?) | **des / eines** (+noun -s) |

## 🔁 ADJECTIVE ENDINGS CHEAT SHEET

### After definite articles (*der/die/das*)

| Case | Masc. | Fem. | Neut. | Plural |
|------|-------|------|-------|--------|
| Nom. | -e | -e | -e | -en |
| Akk. | -en | -e | -e | -en |
| Dat. | -en | -en | -en | -en |
| Gen. | -en | -en | -en | -en |

**Example:** *der alt**e** Mann* (Nom.) → *den alt**en** Mann* (Akk.) → *dem alt**en** Mann* (Dat.)

### After indefinite articles (*ein/eine/ein*)

| Case | Masc. | Fem. | Neut. | Plural |
|------|-------|------|-------|--------|
| Nom. | -er | -e | -es | -e |
| Akk. | -en | -e | -es | -e |
| Dat. | -en | -en | -en | -en |
| Gen. | -en | -en | -en | -en |

💬 **Commentary:** A useful trick — after *ein/eine/ein*, the adjective "takes over" the strong ending that the article *doesn't* show. For example: *ein* (no gender signal) + *alt**er** Mann* — the adjective carries the -er to signal masculine.

## 🧩 PRACTICE SENTENCES — Fill in the blank

Try to identify the correct case, then choose the right article/ending.

1. Ich schenke **___ Mutter** (fem.) Blumen. *(Dative — indirect object)*
2. Er kauft **___ neuen Wagen** (masc.). *(Accusative — direct object)*
3. Das ist **___ Haus ___ Lehrers** (masc.). *(Nominative + Genitive)*
4. Wir wohnen **in ___ Stadt** (fem., location). *(Dative — two-way prep., location)*
5. **___ Hund** (masc.) bellt laut. *(Nominative — subject)*

<details>
<summary>✅ Answers</summary>

1. **der** Mutter (Dat. fem.)
2. **einen** neuen Wagen (Akk. masc.)
3. Das ist **das** Haus **des** Lehrers (Nom. + Gen. masc.)
4. in **der** Stadt (Dat. fem.)
5. **Der** Hund (Nom. masc.)

</details>
